Asana's free Personal plan caps at two users. Any account created on or after November 12, 2025 gets two seats instead of the ten older accounts still enjoy. Trello's free tier stops at ten collaborators per workspace. Airtable's free plan allows five editors. If your organization runs on eight staff and a rotating group of volunteers, the phrase "free plan available" has stopped meaning much.

Almost every list of nonprofit project management tools online is published by a company that sells one, or by an affiliate site paid on referral. We don't sell software. At GoodEWorkers, we are a distributed community that builds digital projects for nonprofits and releases the work as open source. We have never had an office, and we build a different team for each project depending on who is free that month. That makes us a harsh test case for any collaboration platform. Everything below is something we use, have used, or have watched a partner organization abandon.

Below: what the free plans really include, which discounts are worth the paperwork, how to get volunteers into a project without buying them a permanent seat, and the open source options nobody else bothers to list.

## Key takeaways

- Project management software is priced per seat, not per hour of use. That one decision is why most free plans break as soon as a nonprofit tries to bring volunteers into a project.
- Under about five active people and three projects, nothing beats a shared document and a weekly review. Learning a new platform costs hours, and those hours come out of program work.
- Nonprofit discounts are worth having and slow to get: 50% off Asana's paid plans, around 30% at Zoho Projects and Miro, all subject to verified 501(c)(3) status or the local equivalent.
- Self-hosted open source removes the seat limit. It does not remove the cost. It moves the cost to whoever on your team can restart a container on a Sunday evening.

## The real cost of a project management tool "free" plan

Vendors bill for active seats, not for activity. A volunteer who spends six hours on a fundraising campaign consumes exactly the same seat as a full-time program manager, and that seat stays consumed until someone remembers to remove them. For a business with a stable headcount this is a reasonable model. For a nonprofit whose working team expands and contracts around events, grants deadlines and campaigns, it is the wrong shape.

### What changed in 2025 and 2026 for nonprofit project management tools

The direction of travel is one way. Asana cut its free tier from ten seats to two in November 2025. Microsoft stopped granting free Business Premium licences for renewals after July 2025, leaving Business Basic as the free offer and a discount on everything above it. Several guides still circulating online quote fifteen free Asana users, a number that has not been accurate for years.

Then there is seat bundling, which almost no comparison mentions. Asana sells seats individually between two and five users, then in blocks of five. A team of six pays for ten. If you plan a rollout across a small organization, the jump from five to six people is not one seat, it is five.

### The discounts you have to apply for

Almost every major platform offers a nonprofit rate, and almost none of them apply it automatically. You will need a registered charity number or 501(c)(3) determination letter, and usually verification through a third party such as TechSoup or Goodstack. Budget an afternoon for the paperwork and a week or two for approval.

Public bodies, political organizations and grantmaking foundations are shut out of several programs, including Canva's. And a social enterprise structured as a Community Interest Company or a benefit corporation will usually fail eligibility checks written for registered charities, even though the work is identical.

The two big suites give you the most for the least paperwork. Google for Nonprofits and Microsoft 365 for Nonprofits each give a verified organization email on its own domain, shared documents, video calls, file storage and team chat. Check what they already cover before you buy anything dedicated. Plenty of small teams find that shared drives, a calendar and a weekly call handle the planning fine.

## You might not need a tool at all

This is the section the rest of the internet will not write for you, because the rest of the internet is selling something.

Every platform has an adoption cost. Someone has to set it up, define the columns, decide what a task is, invite people, and then chase the three team members who never open it. In a small organization that person is usually the executive director, and the hours come straight out of program work. The tool only pays for itself once the coordination it replaces was expensive enough to hurt.

Our rule: under five active people and three projects running at once, don't adopt anything. A shared document, one writing rule and a fifteen-minute weekly review will beat any platform at that size. Everyone already knows how to use a document.

In practice:

- **One document per project, one owner.** Not a folder, not a chat thread. One page that holds the goal, the decisions made so far, and the open items with a name and a date against each.
- **A writing rule everyone follows.** Every task gets written down before work starts, with who is doing it and by when. This is the single habit that separates teams who stay organized from teams who hold meetings about being disorganized.
- **A weekly review, fifteen minutes.** Walk the open items, update the status of each, close what is done, and move the rest. Nothing else.

We use exactly this for small projects, and we run entirely asynchronously across time zones. The discipline is in the writing, not the software. A team that writes things down will work well in a text file; a team that doesn't will be equally lost in ClickUp.

The moment to adopt a real tool is when you can name the specific failure it fixes. Deadlines being missed because nobody could see them. Two people doing the same work. A volunteer who could not start because nobody could tell them what was already done. Those are tool-shaped problems. "We feel disorganized" is not, and buying a platform to fix a feeling is how organizations end up paying monthly for something nobody opens.

## The volunteer seat problem

Nonprofits rarely have a team. They have a core of staff and a periphery that moves: skilled volunteers who give a few hours, board members who appear before meetings, a designer who helps for three weeks and then goes back to their paid work.

Every per-seat pricing model is built on the opposite assumption. It assumes a stable list of people who log in most days. When a graphic designer joins for one campaign, your organization either pays for a seat that sits idle afterwards, or someone remembers to remove them, which nobody ever does. We have watched small teams pay for a plan sized to the largest number of people who ever touched it, rather than the number who actually use it.

### Four workarounds, and what each costs

**Guest access.** The cleanest option where it exists. Asana's paid plans include unlimited free guest seats, so an external collaborator can see and comment on the projects you share with them without adding to your licence count. Trello and ClickUp offer comparable limited-access roles. The cost is that guests usually cannot create projects or see the wider workspace, which makes them dependent on someone inside for anything structural.

**Rotating licences.** Buy fewer seats than you have people and reassign them as work moves. It works, and it is administratively tedious: someone has to track who holds what, and removing a user can affect task assignment history depending on the platform. Realistic for one person managing under fifteen contributors, painful above that.

**A read-only board.** Keep the working platform small and paid, and publish a public or link-shared view for everyone else. Trello does this well. Volunteers see priorities and progress without a login; comments and updates come back through email or chat. The cost is a communication gap: people who cannot update their own tasks stop feeling responsible for them.

**Self-hosted software with no seat limit.** Removes the problem at the root. The cost moves to hosting and maintenance, which we cover further down.

### What we do

GoodEWorkers is the extreme version of this problem. We have no office, no salaried staff on projects, and we assemble a different team for each brief depending on who is available. Our working rule is that the tool is never the source of truth for a new contributor: the written brief is. Everything a volunteer needs to start — the goal, the scope, what has already been decided, what is explicitly out of scope — lives in a document they can read before they are given access to anything.

That one habit is what makes onboarding cheap. A contributor reads the brief, picks up a task, and their first day is productive rather than spent asking questions. It also means access to the platform is a convenience rather than a prerequisite, which takes the pressure off the seat count entirely.

This matters beyond cost. NCVO's research keeps finding that the main reason people stop volunteering is lack of time. Every hour a skilled volunteer spends working out what they are supposed to do is an hour that makes them less likely to come back.

### Project management tools for teams under 10

**Trello** remains the honest answer for most small nonprofits, and it is the one we recommend most often. The model is a board, a list, and a card you drag from one column to the next. A volunteer coordinator can learn it in ten minutes without training, which matters more than any feature comparison. The free plan gives ten collaborators and ten boards per workspace, enough for an event, a fundraising campaign and a social media calendar running side by side. Power-Ups add calendar views, automation through Butler, and integrations with Google Drive and Slack.

What breaks: Trello has no real reporting. Once you need to see progress across several projects at once, or report to a funder on where a grant-funded workstream stands, you will be building that view by hand in a spreadsheet. Cards are also easy to move by accident, and there is no strong undo culture.

Verdict: start here unless you already know why it won't work.

**Asana** is the more structured platform, and the one to choose when work actually moves between people: a marketing task that hands off to a designer, then to an approver, then to a scheduler. Custom fields, rules and dependencies make that flow visible instead of verbal. The 50% nonprofit discount brings Starter to roughly $5.50 per user per month annually, which is reasonable for a staff team of six or eight.

What breaks: the free plan is no longer usable by an organization. Two seats is a personal tool. And the seat-bundling rule means growing from five to six people costs you five seats, not one. Budget for the step, not the increment.

Verdict: skip the free plan entirely. On the discounted paid plan, the unlimited guest seats make it one of the better options for volunteer-heavy work.

**Google Workspace for Nonprofits** is not project management software, and it is still the first thing to set up. A verified organization gets email on its own domain, Docs and Sheets for real-time collaboration, Drive, Meet, Chat and a shared calendar, free, for up to 2,000 users. For a team of five running two projects, a shared Sheet with a status column and a Calendar with the deadlines on it is a complete system. It costs nothing, and nobody needs training.

Verdict: the baseline every other tool on this page has to beat.

### PM tools best suited for data-heavy programs

**Airtable** sits between a spreadsheet and a database, and it is the right answer when your projects are really records: a donor list, a volunteer roster, a grant pipeline with deadlines attached, a case log. You can build a custom view for each audience, link records across tables, and automate routine steps. The free plan allows unlimited bases, 1,000 records per base, five editors, 1GB of attachments and 100 automation runs per month.

What breaks: Airtable is only as good as the person who built it, and it becomes fragile when that person leaves. We have seen more than one organization inherit a beautifully complex base that nobody else can safely modify. If you build in Airtable, document the structure.

Verdict: excellent for structured data, risky as a single point of knowledge.

**Notion** is a documentation platform with task tracking attached, rather than the reverse. For a written-first team it is very strong: meeting notes, policies, project briefs and a task database can all live in one place, and the ability to write properly inside the tool is rare. Its AI can draft and summarize, which saves some typing.

What breaks: as a pure tracker it is slower than a dedicated board, and permissions get complicated once external collaborators are involved. Teams that want a simple list of who is doing what often find it does too much.

Verdict: choose it if your culture is writing, not if your problem is scheduling.

**Zoho Projects** is the quiet value option. It offers Gantt charts, time tracking, task dependencies and reporting at roughly a third of what comparable platforms charge, with an additional nonprofit discount of around 30% on paid plans. For an organization that needs structured planning and cannot justify Monday.com pricing, it is the obvious choice.

What breaks: the interface feels a generation behind, and the ease of use gap shows during onboarding. Expect to spend more time training people than you would on Trello.

Verdict: the best features-per-dollar on this list, at a cost in polish.

### Project management tools best suited for multi-program organizations

**ClickUp** aims to replace everything: tasks, docs, goals, reminders, calendars, chat. Its free plan is unusually generous, with unlimited members and unlimited tasks, capped mainly on storage at 100MB. In principle that solves the volunteer seat problem outright, which is why it appears on so many nonprofit lists.

What breaks: the learning curve is real and the default configuration overwhelms new users. Unlimited members only helps if those members can find anything. Plan for someone to own the setup and simplify it aggressively before anyone else is invited.

Verdict: the most capable free plan here, and the one most likely to be abandoned in month three.

**Monday.com** is the most visually satisfying platform on this list and the easiest to demo to a board. Customizable workflows, colour-coded status columns, dashboards that make performance legible at a glance, and integrations with Gmail and dozens of other tools. Marketing and communications teams tend to love it.

What breaks: the free plan is too limited to be useful for an organization, and pricing is charged in seat tiers that push you to buy more seats than you need. For a nonprofit, it is usually the right tool at the wrong price.

Verdict: worth it only if visual clarity is the specific problem you are solving.

**Celoxis** and comparable portfolio platforms belong in a different conversation. They combine multi-project scheduling, resource allocation, budget tracking and cross-project reporting, and they make sense for an organization running several concurrent funded programs where a funder expects to see costs and timelines in one place. There is no free plan and pricing is quoted.

Verdict: relevant above roughly thirty staff and multiple restricted grants. Below that, overkill.

## Open source and self-hosted options

No other guide to nonprofit project management tools covers this, which is strange given that the sector's two loudest concerns are cost and control of data.

Open source software removes the licence fee and the seat limit. You can give access to thirty volunteers without a pricing conversation. It does not remove the cost; it converts a predictable monthly invoice into an unpredictable maintenance obligation.

### When self-hosting makes sense, and when it doesn't

The test is one question. Is there someone in your organization who can restart a Docker container on a Sunday evening? Not someone technical in the abstract. Someone reachable, who knows where the server lives, and who will still be around in eighteen months. If the answer is no, don't self-host. A fundraising campaign that stalls because the board is offline and the only person who understands the setup is on holiday costs more than any subscription.

A realistic budget for a small self-hosted instance is a virtual server at roughly $10 to $20 per month, plus a domain, plus an hour or two of someone's time each month for updates and backups. If you value that time at anything, self-hosting is cheaper than a paid platform above roughly eight to ten users, and more expensive below it.

Most organizations should take the middle path instead. Several open source projects offer a managed cloud version at a modest monthly price, often with a nonprofit rate. You get the licence freedom and the data portability without running a server.

### The options worth knowing

- **Vikunja** — a clean task and project manager with list, kanban, Gantt and table views. Close to the simplicity of Trello, with no user limit. Available self-hosted or as a hosted plan.
- **Plane** — a modern issue and project tracker aimed at product teams, increasingly used more broadly. Good cycles and roadmaps, actively developed, cloud option available.
- **OpenProject** — the most complete of the group, with Gantt charts, budgets, time tracking and a dedicated community edition. Heavier to run and to learn, but genuinely full-featured.
- **Kanboard** — deliberately minimal kanban. Tiny resource footprint, runs on almost anything, no learning curve. If all you need is boards, this is the least fragile option here.
- **Wekan** — an open source board tool with a familiar card interface, easy for anyone who has used Trello.
- **Nextcloud Deck** — if your organization already runs Nextcloud for file storage, Deck adds kanban boards with no extra infrastructure at all. The cheapest route to self-hosted project management by a wide margin.
- **Focalboard** — a project and task board that can run locally or self-hosted, suited to smaller teams.
- **Leantime** — built with non-technical teams in mind, combining strategy, planning and task management in one place.

Licences, hosting offers and maintenance activity move quickly in this part of the market. Check each project's repository for recent commits, and its own site for current hosted pricing, before you commit.

### Data ownership and getting out

The argument for open source is not only financial. A nonprofit holding information about beneficiaries, donors or service users has obligations that do not disappear because the software was free. Two questions to ask of any platform, commercial or open.

Can you export everything, in a usable format, today? Not a PDF report. A structured export of tasks, comments, attachments and history that another system could import. Test it before you depend on the platform, not afterwards.

And what happens if the price doubles? Vendors raise prices and retire free tiers, as Asana and Microsoft both did recently. An organization that can export and move has a negotiating position. One that cannot has a budget line that grows on someone else's schedule.

This is why we release what we build as open source. It is not a philosophical preference so much as a practical one: the organizations we work with should still own their tools after we stop being involved.

## How to choose: the adoption test

Every comparison you will read scores platforms on features. Features are the wrong measure, because the feature you don't use costs the same as the one you do. The right measure is whether your team will still open the thing in three months.

Five questions, roughly in order of how much they predict success.

- How long until the first useful board exists? Not until the platform is configured. Until a real project is on it and someone has moved a task. If that takes more than an afternoon, the tool is too complex for where your organization is right now.
- How many clicks to create a task? Count them. If capturing a task takes longer than writing it on paper, people will write it on paper, and your single source of truth quietly becomes three competing ones.
- What does it do on a phone, in a bad signal, at an event? Program staff and volunteers are frequently nowhere near a desk. A platform that only works properly on a laptop will lose half your updates.
- How hard is it to leave? Ask before you commit, not after. Export format, what comes with it, what gets lost.
- Who uses it when the person who set it up is unavailable? This is the one that separates tools that last from tools that die. If the answer is nobody, you have built a dependency, not a system.

### A short decision path

- **Fewer than 5 active people, fewer than 3 projects** → don't adopt a platform. Use a shared document, a writing rule and a weekly review.
- **5 to 15 people, mostly staff, simple work** → Trello free, or Google Workspace if the grant has already been approved. Move to Asana on the discounted paid plan when handoffs between people start failing.
- **5 to 15 people, many volunteers coming and going** → a paid plan with free guest seats, or a self-hosted option if you have genuine technical capacity in-house.
- **Program data is the real problem, not task tracking** → Airtable, and document the structure from day one.
- **Multiple funded programs, funder reporting** → a portfolio platform, and budget for someone to run it.

Whatever you choose, set it up for the least confident person on your team rather than the most. The measure of a good setup is not what it can do. It is how little has to be explained.

One last thing, and no software solves it. Sometimes the reason a project is stuck is not coordination but capacity: the website needs rebuilding, the donor emails need writing, the SEO needs fixing, and nobody in the organization has the skills or the hours. That is a different problem, and a better tool will not touch it. If that sounds familiar, that is the gap we exist to fill.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is Asana still free for nonprofits?

Asana's free Personal plan is limited to two users for any account created on or after November 12, 2025. Accounts created before that date keep their older ten-seat allowance. Eligible nonprofits receive 50% off the Starter and Advanced plans, which is the practical route for any organization with more than two people.

### What is the best free project management tool for a small charity?

For most small charities and nonprofits, Trello's free plan is the best starting point: ten collaborators, ten boards, and a drag-and-drop interface that takes about ten minutes to learn. If your organization is already verified for Google Workspace for Nonprofits, a shared Sheet and a team calendar may cover the same need at no cost and with no new software to learn.

### Can volunteers use our project management tool without paying for a seat?

Often, yes. Asana's paid plans include unlimited free guest seats, so external collaborators can work on shared projects without increasing your licence count. Trello and ClickUp offer comparable limited-access roles, and ClickUp's free plan allows unlimited members outright. Self-hosted open source software has no seat limit at all.

### Do we qualify for nonprofit discounts without 501(c)(3) status?

It depends on the program and your country. Most vendors accept an equivalent registration, such as a UK charity number, verified through TechSoup or Goodstack. Public bodies, political organizations and grantmaking foundations are excluded from several programs, and social enterprises structured as CICs or benefit corporations frequently fail eligibility checks written for registered charities.

### Is self-hosted project management software really free?

The licence is free; the running costs are not. Expect around $10 to $20 per month for a small virtual server, plus a domain, plus one to two hours of someone's time each month for updates and backups. Self-hosting becomes cheaper than a paid platform above roughly eight to ten users, provided you have someone who can maintain it reliably.

### What should we do if our tool raises its prices?

Export your data BEFORE you negotiate. Check now whether the platform offers a complete structured export including tasks, comments and attachments, and test it once. An organization that can move has options; one that cannot will pay whatever the next price increase asks for.
